Nils Aall Barricelli's 1954 paper translated

Shared by Lana

Have you heard of Nils Aall Barricelli, the ALife pioneer from the early 1900s who ran evolutionary simulations on military computers in the 1950s? Nautilus published a great article about him back in 2014: Meet the Father of Digital Life.

Giulia Bianchi and Tim Taylor teamed up to translate his 1954 paper “Esempi Numerici di Processi di Evoluzione” (Numerical Examples of Evolutionary Processes) from Italian to English! It is in the ALife journal (closed access): Numerical Examples of Evolutionary Processes or open access here.

Intelligence in Nature — Artificial Life, Emergence, and Computational Form

Shared by Yuvraj Mittal

Portfolio: yuvrajmittal.com

Intelligence in Nature is a collection of four computational artworks exploring how intricate, organic forms emerge from local interactions in artificial-life systems.

Through simulations inspired by fungal networks, cellular growth, and other living processes, the collection explores emergence as both a computational phenomenon and an artistic medium. Rather than depicting biological forms directly, the works allow structures and patterns to develop through simulated dynamics.

Each piece is presented as a moving artwork, accompanied by an explanation of its underlying process, source code, and reproduction instructions.

The collection includes Anastomosis, Interstices, Pelagic, and Inhabited Ground

3D Game of Life

summary by Claus

Last month Imy was looking into 3D versions of the game of life. Out of several projects, Cubic Life looked the best.

(If the animation does not show, you can see it here.)

Each cell looks at its 26 neighbors (+-1 in all 3D directions). A live cell becomes death with less than 5 or more than 6 neighbors. A dead cell becomes alive with exactly 4 neighbors.

The website does not have a lot of information on the hows and whys of these rules, but it is interesting to think how the addition of an extra dimension interacts with the celular automata rules. Personally, I'm curious about why the birth rule ends up being outside the boundaries of the death rule, unlike 2D GoL.

Do you know any interesting materials or theories about 3D GoL? Send us a message!

23 rd Human-Competitive Results Awards (“Humies”), 2026

Shared by Erik Goodman, edited for length by Lana

GECCO 2026 was held July 13-17 this year; around 400 people came to San José, Costa Rica to attend in person, and an additional 200 or so registered to attend virtually. The Humies annually awards $10,000 in cash prizes for computational results that are deemed to be competitive with results produced by humans but are generated automatically by computers. The finals of the Humies competition was a plenary session attended by about 75 people. The six finalists selected from among 9 entries this year presented their work in 10-minute talks.

All entries and the presentation materials of the six finalists are available at www.human-competitive.org. The judges for the competition were Anne Auger, Wolfgang Banzhaf, Stephanie Forrest, Emma Hart, Lee Spector and Darrell Whitley. Publicity for the Humies was done by Bill Langdon.

Video of the Humies session

Gold Award

The Gold, with a $5,000 prize, was awarded to the team from Google headed by (and presented by) Esteban Real. Their paper was entitled “AutoNumerics-Zero: Automated Discovery of State-of-the-Art Mathematical Functions” show that transcendental functions, such as the exponential, can be approximated more efficiently by targeting only the finite precision required by modern computers rather than arbitrary mathematical accuracy. Using symbolic regression, an evolutionary algorithm discovers new combinations of basic arithmetic operations that optimize both accuracy and computational efficiency without relying on prior mathematical knowledge.

Silver Award

The Silver Award and $3,000 went to a team which included Chao Qian (Nanjing University), and fifteen co-authors. Their paper is entitled, “Complex marine ecological response during the Eocene-Oligocene revealed by global foraminiferal record.” Their results show that different ecological groups of foraminifera responded in distinct ways, rather than experiencing a single, uniform extinction event.

Bronze Award

One Bronze Award and $1,000 went to a team from University of Toulouse Capitole, and their presentation was by Christina Berghegger. Their paper was entitled, “Analyzing Interpretable Visual Control Policy Search and Synthesis.” Their study presents an interpretable approach to vision-based sequential decision-making using Graph-based Genetic Programming (GGP) as an alternative to black-box deep reinforcement learning.

Another Bronze Award and $1,000 went to the team of Arinze Obidiegwu et al, from University of Limerick and Atlantic Technological University. Their paper is entitled “Evolving Hardware-Efficient Grover Circuits with Grammatical Evolution”. The paper explores the use of Grammatical Evolution (GE) to automatically design quantum circuits that are optimized for today's noisy quantum hardware. Rather than relying on the standard, general-purpose implementation of Grover's algorithm, the authors evolve specialized circuits for each 3-qubit computational basis state.

The Cognition Project Podcast

Shared by Imy

In case you needed another podcast...

Cognitive science is pervasive in ALife, with many of the field's influential figures and ideas coming from this field. So I found it really cool to see this new podcast that traces the history of cognitive science through oral history interviews with prominent figures in the field. The creator, Tom Griffiths of Princeton University, has developed it in a way that allows it to be adapted or mapped onto undergraduate cognitive science courses, which could be really useful for incoming (or current) students, as well as anybody who is putting such a course together.

From the creator himself:

Over the last decade I have been doing oral history interviews with cognitive scientists and am releasing them in a podcast called the Cognition Project. Currently we have interviews posted with Jerome Bruner, Molly Potter, Susan Carey, Lila Gleitman, Noam Chomsky, Jean Berko Gleason, Tom Bever, Dan Slobin, Richard Atkinson, Gordon Bower, Roger Shepard, Rich Shiffrin, Elizabeth Loftus, Ewart Thomas, Phil Johnson-Laird, Donald Norman, Eleanor Rosch, and Douglas Hofstadter. The interviews so far have focused on the cognitive revolution, mathematical psychology, and the first cognitive science conferences, but we’re moving into a section on different approaches to AI and the origins of neural networks. The interviews are designed to be accessible to undergraduates so they can be assigned in classes where you cover the work of these researchers.

Apple: https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/the-cognition-project/id1872765981

Spotify: https://open.spotify.com/show/7rhwBGhEQCtO9cBguazFsq

It feels like there's an opportunity to do something similar for ALife. Hmm...

Book: Collective Intelligence by Agnieszka Kurant

Shared by Lana

A new book published by MIT press, blurb below.

Conceptual artist Agnieszka Kurant's work alongside newly commissioned texts by renowned thinkers across science, philosophy, technology, anthropology, and economics.

Collective Intelligence is an innovative monograph that documents the last ten years of Polish-born conceptual artist Agnieszka Kurant's interdisciplinary practice. It includes newly commissioned texts by renowned thinkers in science, philosophy, art, technology, anthropology, and economics.

Kurant's experimental work investigates collective and nonhuman intelligences and their impact on transformations of the human, the future of labor and creativity, and the exploitations within digital capitalism. Questioning the ideology of individualism, Kurant proposes that we rethink human and more-than-human worlds from a perspective of plural subjectivity, and, through this fundamental shift in perspective, posits the possibility of alternative political imaginaries. Her work probes the replacement of individual authorship with collective intelligence—a phenomenon observed in slime molds, termite colonies, social movements, cities, the internet, and inside our brains.

In her collaborative practice, the artist investigates artificial intelligence, emergence, cybernetics, automation, artificial life, mining industries, and energy circuits to explore our collective evolution and the shifting status of objects in relation to agency, value, circulation, and redistribution. Through crowdsourcing the production of her artworks to thousands of humans and nonhumans, Kurant creates unstable, hybrid forms that constantly evolve. Her works, oscillating between biological, digital, and geological, embody the crumbling distinctions between what is natural and artificial, real and synthetic, and life and nonlife.

Dispersed throughout the book, the Phenomena section adopts a quasi-encyclopedic format to highlight and expand on Kurant's research on collective intelligence.

Seminars

Teaching in Interdisciplinary Settings: The hows, whens, and whys? (October 29th)

The ALife-Edu group is hosting a virtual talk titled Teaching in Interdisciplinary Settings: The hows, whens, and whys? by Amanda Jean Bowerman on Oct 29th at 9AM EDT. The talk will be at this Zoom link and the abstract is below. We'll be recording and sharing that for those who can't make the time.

Interdisciplinarity is becoming a fundamental element of both academia and industry, as fields like data science, cognitive science, and artificial life are becoming more prevalent. These types of fields are appealing to undergraduate students by offering the ability to integrate knowledge across various domains of interest. However, teaching in interdisciplinary spaces has proven to be challenging, as undergraduate students are still developing disciplinary knowledge coupled with the varying meanings for terminology across disciplines. There has been some recent research into investigating how to facilitate conversations in interdisciplinary settings in addition to developing a toolkit and resources for designing interdisciplinary courses. Flipped classrooms have been instrumental in the integration of interdisciplinarity, providing students the ability to interact through discussion and promoting active learning. In this talk, I will discuss how to handle some of the challenges of teaching in an interdisciplinary setting, drawing insights from my own experiences teaching in interdisciplinary intro-level data science courses while also providing resources and suggestions for implementing interdisciplinary domains in a flipped classroom setting.
